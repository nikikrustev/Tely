# 36 — How blind and low-vision fans follow football today, and what they say they want

_Accessed/compiled: 20 Oct 2026 · Priority area: Explain + Render + Personalise (Audio Stands: description grammar, sync, verbosity, talk-back)_

## TL;DR

- **The fans are clear about what they want.** RNIB's 2022 research ran 10 interviews plus two focus groups. Fans want **one voice that describes the ball in real time**. That voice says where the ball is (third, side, near or far), what kind of pass it was, which way the shot went, and what the keeper did. It describes both kits and the players' build, and saves punditry for stoppages. A normal TV commentary scored **6.1 out of 15** on RNIB's scale (enjoyment + access + ability to follow). Fans disliked description that arrives after the action (backfilling).
- **Lag is the biggest technical complaint.** Fans hear the crowd react before they hear what happened. Three of the 20 Premier League clubs ran audio description on a 30 s delay (LPF survey, 2015), and DAB radio or phone streams add more lag. One RNIB focus-group fan said "even a one second delay can make a huge difference". In Audio Stands, the crowd and the commentary are generated **from the same event stream**, so they can be in perfect sync. That is our biggest structural advantage.
- **Today's services are one-way and the same for everyone.** That covers club headsets, radio, Sky's new Live Descriptive Audio Commentary (from 10 Oct 2026), the UEFA and FIFA apps, and the tactile tablets (Touch2See, Field of Vision, OneCourt). None of them lets the fan **ask** "where is it?", turn the detail up or down, or set the crowd-vs-voice mix. Tablet testers asked for a radio link to fill gaps in the information.
- **Audio Stands design rules (ours):**
  - ball-first, location-first grammar
  - a zero-lag crowd generated from the same events
  - three verbosity levels
  - "where is it?" and "what did I miss?" on demand
  - talk-back answered in stoppages
  - lookahead used for **build-up**, never to give away the outcome
- **Testing:** use remote, paid, one-to-one sessions with screen-reader users, repeating RNIB's two-clip A/B design and its 15-point scale. If we cannot test before the deadline, say so plainly (wording below).

## Findings

### 1. Scale and context

- **Prevalence.** The WHO puts near or distance vision impairment at at least 2.2 billion people (fact sheet, Feb 2026). RNIB says more than 2 million people in the UK live with sight loss, and about 320,000 are registered sight impaired or severely sight impaired.
- **Disabled fans' survey.** The Level Playing Field (LPF) 2025 survey had 2,151 responses. Of these, 8% reported a sight disability and 5% said they would benefit from audio-descriptive commentary (ADC). Stadium technology was a barrier for 13%, and anxiety or lack of confidence for 22.5%.
- **Not found.** We found no Ofcom or SGSA survey specific to blind fans' commentary.
- **Radio history.** In 1927 the BBC published an eight-square pitch grid in the Radio Times. The commentator called out the square the ball was in. It was an early "where is the ball" protocol (our framing). The popular story that the grid gave us the phrase back to square one is disputed.
- **Sound in blind sport.** In blind football (5-a-side, IBSA rules) the ball is audible. A guide stands behind the goal and spectators are asked to stay silent during play (rulebook and secondary summaries; spectator-silence wording not found in the rulebook itself). Tony Stockman, a former GB blind-football player, co-wrote an ICAD 2007 paper on audio-only football. It notes that echolocation helps players sense the pitch. It also leaves real-time sonification of ball and player positions as an open problem. RNIB uses goalball as its example of shot direction (straight or across, which wing) needing to be described. **Lesson for us:** location and direction carry the meaning, and silence and crowd sound are both information.

### 2. What an AD commentator does differently

| Technique | Source |
|---|---|
| **Ball location first, player name second.** Map the pitch in thirds (defensive, middle, attacking), then near or far side and left or right. Avoid vague phrases such as high up the pitch or inside left | Bohemians FC case study (JoSTrans 45, 2026); Shamrock Rovers trainers |
| State **which way each team is kicking**, and repeat it after half-time | LPF on Luton Town's ADC; RNIB focus group 1 (kits, formation and direction described at kick-off) |
| **Pass type** (chipped or along the ground), **shot direction** (left, right, middle; wide or over), **keeper response** | RNIB TV report recs 3–4; RNIB best practice §3 |
| **Off-ball shape**: for example, two defenders in front and one behind. Fans asked for more on players off the ball and liked hearing about switches of play | RNIB TV report |
| **Both kits**: colours, patterns, boots. Also player build (tall, strong, fast) and hair or tattoos | RNIB TV report rec 5; RNIB best practice §2; Bohemians |
| **Body language, facial expression, scenery, clothing, atmosphere**: banners, flares, crowd | CAFE wording via UEFA (2018); RNIB best practice §2 |
| **Avoid** statistics, tactics and long recaps during play | CAFE via UEFA (2018); RNIB stadium guidance (2023) |
| At a goal, **describe the play first, then name the scorer** | UEFA EURO 2024 ADC commentator |
| **Read out on-screen text**: score, line-ups, stats, VAR. Fans said nobody describes the offside lines during a VAR check | RNIB best practice §1; RNIB focus group 2 |
| Say **ball out of play** before going into background detail | RNIB focus group 1 |
| **Mix commentary above the crowd** | RNIB best practice §7 |
| **Pace**: keep up with play without overwhelming. Some fans find AD too frantic and want short pauses | RNIB TV report rec 6; focus group 1 |

**CAFE.** The Centre for Access to Football in Europe runs an ADC Network and Training Programme. The programme sets training levels and gives broadcast guidance. It can be downloaded in 7 languages. CAFE has trained more than 100 commentators in 30 countries since 2018. EURO 2020 ADC covered 51 matches for €192,500 (Zero Project). Bohemians' first describer had two days of CAFE training, later cut to a three-hour session.

**Not found.** We could not find CAFE's guide document itself (unverified beyond these summaries). We also found **no football AD source that uses clock-face positions**. Clock positions are standard in orientation-and-mobility training, but football AD uses thirds plus sides.

### 3. Pain points fans report

1. **Delay vs crowd.** The crowd roars before the description arrives (LPF 2015 survey; RNIB 2023 guidance on DAB and phone lag). In pubs, radio and pictures drift out of sync (RNIB focus group 1). NHK's 2021 user study found dissatisfaction rose when descriptions lagged the on-air sound (IEICE abstract, unverified detail).
2. **Not knowing where the ball is.** One fan in focus group 1 said they never knew where the ball was during a BBC One clip.
3. **Missing non-ball moments.** Fans miss VAR checks, off-ball positions, ceremonies and displays (RNIB focus groups).
4. **Too much talk or too little.** TV pundits go off topic. A traditional describer who fits into the gaps comes in after the event. Dead silences, as in judo, leave fans lost. Some fans find full AD tiring (RNIB TV report; focus groups; best practice).
5. **Isolation.** Most focus-group participants watch alone in order to follow the game, although they would like to watch with others (RNIB focus group 1). LPF's chair links inaccessibility to isolation (2025). Bulky headsets that look like ear defenders mark fans out, and fans want to blend in (RNIB 2023).
6. **Uneven provision.** Coverage is patchy: no AD at away grounds, no AD at women's games, and receivers that are not charged (RNIB 2023 checklist). In 2015, only **4 of 20** PL clubs had trained in-house describers and 13 just relayed radio (LPF). We found **no current count** of PL clubs with ADC. Alan March Sport supplied 11 UK clubs as of Oct 2022.
7. **Fans still want personality.** They want an expert star voice for insight, but only at the start, half-time and the end (RNIB focus group 2; TV report rec 2).

### 4. Existing products and their limits

| Product | How it works | Limits (for our differentiation) |
|---|---|---|
| Club ADC (e.g. Arsenal, Newcastle, Wolves) | Trained human describers broadcast to receivers or headsets. CAFE venues use an FM frequency in the stadium (Zero Project) | Only in the stadium. Quality varies with the volunteer. One stream for everyone. Equipment has to be collected and charged |
| Radio (BBC 5 Live etc.) | Human commentary | Not written for blind fans. Drifts against TV and the crowd. Adds off-topic chat (RNIB focus group 1) |
| **Sky Live Descriptive Audio Commentary** | From 10 Oct 2026, about 109 PL games a season, radio-style. Co-funded by Guinness, evaluated by RNIB | TV only. One-way. No user control |
| UEFA EURO 2024 app ADC | All 51 matches streamed remotely in the official app. Team languages plus German or English | One-way. Tournament only |
| FIFA World Cup 2026 | "FIFA Audio Description" app (EN/FR/ES). Haptic boards at select matches in 4 stadiums | One-way. Few boards |
| **Touch2See** (Toulouse) | Tablet with a magnetic puck that follows the ball. Vibration strength shows importance. **Automatically generated** audio for time, score and fouls. Ligue 1 tests at Toulouse and the Vélodrome from Jan 2023, Cagliari, Africa Cup of Nations, St. Louis CITY pilot (15 Mar 2025, 10 fans). **PSG use not found (unverified)** | Needs stadium cameras or data and 5G. The team or league pays (B2B). Testers asked for it to be lighter and more interactive. Users of similar tablets wanted the radio linked in |
| **Field of Vision** (Dublin) | Handheld device with a magnetic ring. Computer-vision cameras track the ball. Haptics, alongside audio commentary. Pilots at Aviva Stadium 2025, Club World Cup demos 2025, Bohemians | Leased hardware. Pilot scale. Safety concern from a Bohemians user about holding a device during goal celebrations |
| OneCourt (US) | Tactile board for the NBA and MLB | Some devices disconnected for a whole half. Fans want it linked to the radio. Experts warn that startup devices get announced and then abandoned (CNBC/NBC, Apr 2025) |
| Action Audio / HawkAR (tennis) | Hawk-Eye tracking turned into a 3D soundscape. Wimbledon 2026 Centre Court on BBC iPlayer AD. Co-designed with blind and low-vision users | Tennis only. No speech Q&A |
| NHK automatic AD | Event metadata goes to a text generator and then TTS. Used for more than 2,000 contests at Rio 2016 | Fills gaps in the main commentary. Not interactive |
| Research: "Did you see that!?" (INTERACT 2021, n=12) | Tennis: AD gives placement, 3D audio gives direction, radio gives the human voice | Radio alone was overwhelming. People want to watch alone **and** with friends |

**The gap Audio Stands fills.** No existing service combines four things:
- (a) a talk-back AI commentator
- (b) user-set verbosity and audio mix
- (c) a crowd bed driven by the same events, so it is in sync by construction
- (d) lookahead-shaped build-up

Haptics (Heartbeat) runs on a phone the fan already owns, so no tablet has to be leased.

## So what for us

**Audio Stands. These are our design principles, derived from the findings above.**

1. **Explain stage: ball-first grammar.** Each live line follows this order: location (third, side), team colour, action, then player name if there is time. Example: "Midfield, far side, Reds; chipped forward…". At kick-off and half-time, give the kits (both), the direction of play and a one-line stadium scene. Name the scorer only *after* describing the goal.
2. **Render stage: one clock for crowd and voice.** Generate the Home End crowd bed and the commentary from the same timed cues (research 24). The crowd may never react before the description starts. With lookahead, we can begin the *build-up* intonation about 4–8 s before a big chance (our number, untested). The **outcome** is withheld until the event's timecode. Heartbeat haptics ramp up on the same tension signal (research 23 and 09).
3. **Personalise stage: three verbosity levels**, switchable by voice or key:
   - **Describer** (continuous, includes off-ball shape)
   - **Radio** (ball and key moments, short pauses)
   - **Calm** (goals, chances, cards, score every 5 min; haptics carry the rest)

   This addresses both complaints: fans who find AD too frantic, and fans who get too little.
4. **Talk-back without losing the play.** "Where is it?" and "what's the score?" are answered at once, in 1–2 short sentences. Longer questions ("why did they sub him?") are queued until the ball is out of play, and the fan is told that. Say "ball's out" before any background (RNIB focus group 1). Use Voice Live barge-in plus function calls (research 27).
5. **Off-ball layer on request.** Offer shape ("three at the back, Blues' left-back very high"), numbers in front of and behind the ball, VAR checks with a description of the line, subs and celebrations.
6. **Read every overlay.** Any cue that has on-screen text must also carry a `speak` string. This links to research 32's `kind="descriptions"` track.
7. **Mix control.** Give the fan a crowd-vs-commentary slider. Commentary always ducks the crowd to stay intelligible (RNIB best practice §7), and there is a "stadium only" mode for goals.
8. **A pundit by appointment.** One describing voice in play. A second "expert" voice only at kick-off, half-time and full time.
9. **Social and safety.** Offer a shared "Audio for All" mode that sighted friends can also listen to (Cambridge United). Make it work on ordinary earbuds. Haptics must be optional and must not require holding the phone.
10. **Honesty.** Use stock AI voices, labelled as AI (research 27). It is synthetic match data, so say so at the start.

Other ideas, briefly:
- **C One Number** and **13 Recap Dial** reuse the verbosity levels.
- **12 Jargon Buster** explains terms on first use (RNIB best practice §6).
- **B Speed Trap** must speak its thresholds, not just show them.
- **F Graphics Desk** gets a "speak" column for every cue.

## Reuse: code & links

Position phrase from synthetic event coordinates. This assumes x runs 0–105 m along the pitch and y runs 0–68 m from the near (camera) touchline; adapt it to research 02.

```python
def where(x: float, y: float, attacking_to_high_x: bool, team: str, colour: str) -> str:
    """Ball-first AD phrase, from the possessing team's point of view."""
    ax = x if attacking_to_high_x else 105 - x           # distance from own goal line
    third = "defensive third" if ax < 35 else "midfield" if ax < 70 else "attacking third"
    side = "near side" if y < 22.7 else "far side" if y > 45.3 else "centre"
    if ax > 88.5 and 13.85 < y < 54.15:                  # inside the penalty area
        third, side = "in the box", ("near post side" if y < 34 else "far post side")
    return f"{colour} ({team}), {third}, {side}"

VERBOSITY = {  # which cue kinds are spoken at each level (ours)
    "describer": {"pass", "carry", "shape", "duel", "shot", "goal", "card", "sub", "var", "crowd"},
    "radio":     {"pass", "shot", "goal", "card", "sub", "var"},
    "calm":      {"shot", "goal", "card", "var", "score_update"},
}

def speakable(cue: dict, level: str) -> bool:
    return cue["kind"] in VERBOSITY[level] or cue.get("priority") == "critical"
```

Lookahead build-up rule (ours): `if next_event.kind in {"shot","penalty"} and next_event.xg >= 0.15: start_tension(at=next_event.t - 6s)`. This raises the commentator's prosody, the crowd murmur and the haptic rate. Never emit outcome text before `next_event.t`.

### Testing with blind users: ethical and quick

- **Format.** Run remote one-to-one sessions of 30–45 min on Teams or Zoom, on the participant's own device and screen reader. Take consent verbally. Pay people for their time. Let them pause their screen reader so you can hear them (NHS Digital remote accessibility research, 2021). RNIB ran its 10 interviews on Teams, at 60–90 min each.
- **Protocol.** Copy RNIB's design. Play two 3-minute synthetic clips, Describer vs Radio (or ours vs plain TV-style). Then ask comprehension probes: "Where was the free kick from? Which way did the shot go? Who scored?" Score each clip on RNIB's 15-point scale (enjoyment, access, following) and compare with RNIB's 6.1/15 baseline for TV. Finally, ask fans to try "where is it?" live.
